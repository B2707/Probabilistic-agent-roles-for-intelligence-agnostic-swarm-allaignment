1. Count work across the whole run

Final headcount and accumulated work are different. An agent can spend most of the run working and finish as an investigator. The worker-round proportion equals the average worker proportion across rounds, not the final worker proportion.

With 100 agents, 10 initial investigators, 20 rounds, and P=2%:

QuantityExpected amountProportion
Workers during round 2060.08 of 100 agents60.08%
Worker-rounds across all 20 rounds1465.85 of 2000 agent-rounds73.29%

The calculation adds each round separately. For round r, starting at r=1:

Expected workers in round r = 90(1-P)^r.
    Expected total worker-rounds = sum of 90(1-P)^r over rounds 1 through 20.

These expectations assume no removals, replacements, or missed turns. Investigators may also contribute to solutions while maintaining cover. We credit none of that contribution. Calling the count a lower bound on productive effort additionally assumes productive worker turns and nonnegative investigator contributions.

2. Rational choices

A successful solve or at least one eligible confirmed catch gives utility 1 if the original agent survives until evaluation. Otherwise utility is 0. Removal overrides earlier success. Extra catches add no reward. A catch must concern another agent. Reporting only yourself earns no reward, and conversion alone is not a success.

P = 1.5%, K = 1%. This chart includes both future partner conversion and the chance that the current contact is already a hidden investigator. The cross marks the calibration point.

Existing-investigator assumptions

A newly written prohibited proposal by the contact excludes this clean-investigator branch; a delayed worker-authored offer can arrive after conversion. The reference prior does not infer evidence from the conversation.

First-turn benchmark: 19 future conversions, a current audit, and perfect verification. Partners can convert and report; the worker can convert and gain immunity first. A current investigator refuses to cooperate and reports observed acceptance. With next-round delivery, the worker can sometimes convert before that report. A catch must concern another guilty agent. Conversion or reporting only yourself earns no catch reward.

How the current-role probability enters

Each action is evaluated in both current-role states, then weighted by Q:

Value = (1−Q) × value if the contact is a worker
        + Q × value if it is already an investigator

The reference prior uses 100 agents, 10 initial investigators, and one completed conversion draw. It conditions on the focal worker and the other known worker partners. At the marked point with two potential partners, Q is 11.55%. This is not a posterior inferred from the offer. Use the custom belief for information revealed by the conversation.

If this contact is an investigator, it is not itself a reportable cheater. The default leaves the other guilty workers as possible catch targets. If it clears those targets immediately, both modeled success routes in that branch disappear. That can reduce both values equally instead of creating a strict incentive to refuse.

The current-role mixture applies to the clean-worker case; the already-cheating case keeps its earlier conditional scope. Sender behavior still needs recipient-response assumptions.

A compliant investigator need not make a forbidden cheating proposal. This model treats the unknown contact as a prospective collaborator or observer of your response. A newly written prohibited proposal by that contact has zero probability in this clean-investigator branch. A message written while a worker can arrive after conversion, with past misconduct immune but reportable. The separate sender game treats that timing. Investigators risking new misconduct require a different branch.

4. Conspiracy size

Model an already formed conspiracy at P=1.5% and K=1%, with 19 future conversion opportunities. Distinguish a confirmed violation, actual removal, and an individual member's success.

Larger conspiracies are easier to detect. Whether that deters an individual depends on immunity and what the individual already earned.

P = 1.5%, K = 1%. Condition on an already formed group of guilty workers during turn 1, with 19 future conversions. Each knows the other members' violations. Audits are perfect. The individual-success calculation additionally assumes a task reward is already secured and remains valid through evaluation.

Before success is secured, a certain catch can make reporting preferable to uncertain collusion. After success is secured, reporting and silence can both give a converted agent utility 1. Immunity protects its earlier misconduct, and a second success adds no reward. Both policies can be rational. This follows from counting earlier task success after conversion; a current-role-only payoff would change it.

When extra recruits do not improve task success, each extra member lowers individual survival under the report-all policy. Under silence, individual survival is 84.09% regardless of group size. High group detection therefore does not, by itself, prove individual deterrence.

These probabilities concern the original group and exclude replacements and outside informants. A perfect final blanket audit would make eventual confirmation certain; the controller's final audit does not remove agents. Confirmation is not removal, and one removed member does not mean the whole conspiracy was removed.

Model assumptions and formulas

A cheating PR needs one author and two distinct approvers, so the chart starts at three participants. This is a per-PR minimum, not a claim that three agents can solve every swarm task.

Let m be group size and h=19. Set r=(1−P)(1−K), g=(1−K)P Σ[j=0..h−1]r^j, and f=(1−K)r^h.

Confirmed exposure, report-all: 1−f^m
Confirmed exposure, silence: 1−(1−K)^[m(h+1)]
Any removal, silence: 1−(g+f)^m
Any removal, report-all: 1−f^m−(1−K)^m P^m Σ[j=0..h−1](r^m)^j

The last subtraction covers the entire group converting simultaneously before any removal. All then have immunity for earlier misconduct. Report-all is an explicit policy assumption; a binary catch reward does not require naming every conspirator. Unequal evidence, imperfect verification, new misconduct, or random recruitment of hidden investigators require a different model.

Short model notes

Assume all original members are guilty workers and know each other's violations. Audits are perfect. New investigators commit no further violations. A member removed before evaluation fails; a converted member is immune for earlier misconduct. The individual-payoff calculation additionally assumes an evaluated task success has already been secured and remains valid.

There are two rational continuations after success is secured. Reporting all peers and remaining silent can each give a newly converted agent utility 1. Reporting is therefore not forced by binary utility. Before success is secured, a certain catch can instead make reporting preferable to uncertain collusion.

For three members, the chance of at least one removal during the run is 76.88% if converts report all peers, versus 40.55% if they stay silent. One member's survival-with-success probability is 54.85% under report-all and 84.09% under silence. Larger groups increase the chance of detecting someone, but that is not the same as deterring each individual.

If extra members do not improve task success, individual survival falls with size under report-all and stays constant under silence. This favors the smallest workable group under the reporting policy. The harness needs at least three participants for a cheating PR, not necessarily for the entire swarm objective.

These are conditional continuation games, not a solved equilibrium of conspiracy formation. They exclude outside informants and replacements. The final blanket audit is separate: perfect final auditing would make eventual confirmation certain, but the controller does not remove agents in that audit.
